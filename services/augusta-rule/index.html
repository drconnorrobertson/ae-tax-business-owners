The Augusta Rule: Tax-Free Rental Income from Your Home

IRC Section 280A(g) allows you to rent your personal residence to your business for up to 14 days per year and exclude the rental income entirely from your tax return. Here is how to implement this strategy correctly.

Implement the Augusta Rule

Understanding the Augusta Rule

The Augusta Rule gets its nickname from Augusta, Georgia, home of the Masters golf tournament. Homeowners in Augusta have long rented their homes to visitors during the tournament for premium rates. Congress recognized that requiring these homeowners to report a few days of rental income and track allocable expenses would create an administrative burden disproportionate to the tax revenue involved. The result was IRC Section 280A(g), which provides a straightforward rule: if you rent your home for 14 days or fewer during the tax year, the rental income is not reported on your tax return, and you do not deduct any rental expenses allocable to the rental use.

For business owners, this provision creates a legitimate planning opportunity. If your business has a genuine need to use your home for meetings, retreats, training sessions, holiday parties, or other business events, the business can pay you fair market rent for those days. The business deducts the rent as an ordinary business expense, and you exclude the rental income from your personal return.

How the Tax Benefit Works

The Augusta Rule creates a unique asymmetry in the tax code. From the business side, the rental payment is a deductible business expense under IRC Section 162, reducing the company's taxable income. From the homeowner's side, the rental income is excluded from gross income under IRC Section 280A(g). This means the payment produces a tax deduction without corresponding taxable income.

A Practical Example

A business owner operates an S-Corp and holds 12 business events at her home during the year: monthly board meetings, two strategic planning sessions, a team training day, and the annual company holiday party. Each event requires use of the home for a full day. Based on a fair market rent analysis of comparable event venues in her area, the daily rental rate for her home is $1,500. The total annual rental is $18,000 (12 days at $1,500 per day).

The S-Corp deducts $18,000 as a business expense, reducing the income that passes through to the owner's personal return. The owner receives $18,000 in rental income but does not report it under Section 280A(g). At a combined federal and state marginal tax rate of 40%, the net tax savings is approximately $7,200 per year.

The Augusta Rule can generate $5,000 to $20,000 or more in annual tax savings, depending on the fair market rental value of your home and the number of qualifying business events you hold there (up to 14 days).

What Qualifies as a "Day" of Rental

A rental day is any day during which the home is used for a business purpose and for which rent is paid. Even a partial day counts as one day against the 14-day limit. This means a two-hour board meeting that uses your dining room still consumes one of your 14 rental days. For this reason, it is important to schedule events strategically to maximize the value of each rental day.

Where possible, combine multiple business activities into a single day. For example, a morning board meeting followed by an afternoon strategic planning session and an evening team dinner uses only one rental day, not three. This approach maximizes the per-day rental rate you can charge while staying within the 14-day limit.

Establishing Fair Market Rent

The rental rate charged must reflect what an unrelated third party would pay to use a comparable space for the same purpose. Setting the rate too high invites IRS scrutiny and potential disallowance of the business deduction. Setting it too low means you are leaving legitimate tax savings on the table.

Methods for Determining Fair Market Rent

  • Comparable venue analysis: Research the daily rental rates for comparable event spaces in your area. Look at hotel conference rooms, event venues, co-working spaces, and private dining rooms. Document the rates, square footage, amenities, and location of each comparable.
  • Online rental platforms: Check platforms like Peerspace, VRBO, and Airbnb for daily rental rates of homes similar to yours in your geographic area. These platforms provide transparent, market-based pricing data.
  • Professional appraisal: For homes with high values or unique features, a written fair market rental appraisal from a qualified real estate professional provides the strongest documentation.

We recommend documenting at least three to five comparable properties or venues, noting their size, location, amenities, and daily rates. The rental rate for your home should fall within the range established by these comparables, adjusted for any differences in quality, size, or features.

Documentation Requirements

Proper documentation is essential to withstand an IRS audit. While the Augusta Rule itself is straightforward, the IRS can challenge the arrangement if the documentation is weak or the business purpose is not clearly established. Here is what you need:

Written Rental Agreement

Execute a written rental agreement between you (as the property owner) and your business entity. The agreement should specify the property address, the daily rental rate, the dates of rental, the business purpose for each rental day, and the terms of payment. This agreement should be signed before the first rental event of the year and updated annually.

Business Purpose Documentation

For each rental day, document the specific business purpose served by using the home. This includes:

  • The type of event (board meeting, strategic planning session, team training, client meeting, holiday party, etc.)
  • A meeting agenda or event plan
  • A list of attendees
  • Meeting minutes or notes documenting what was discussed and decided
  • Photographs of the event setup (tables, chairs, presentation materials, catering)

Fair Market Rent Support

Retain your comparable venue analysis, online listing screenshots, or professional appraisal supporting the daily rental rate. This documentation should be prepared before the first rental event, not after the fact during an audit.

Payment Records

The business should pay the rental amount by check or electronic transfer, creating a clear payment trail. Avoid cash payments, which are harder to document. Each payment should reference the rental agreement and the specific dates being paid for.

Documentation is what separates a legitimate Augusta Rule implementation from one that gets disallowed on audit. The rental agreement, business purpose records, fair market rent analysis, and payment records should all be assembled in a single file and retained for at least seven years.

Qualifying Business Events

The key to a defensible Augusta Rule implementation is ensuring that each rental day involves a genuine business activity. The following are common examples of qualifying business events:

  • Board of directors meetings: Every corporation should hold regular board meetings to discuss company performance, strategy, compliance, and major decisions. Holding these meetings at your home is entirely legitimate, especially if the home provides a private and comfortable setting for candid discussions.
  • Strategic planning sessions: Annual or quarterly strategic planning retreats at which the company's direction, goals, and key initiatives are discussed and documented.
  • Team training and development: Training sessions for employees on new procedures, software, safety protocols, or professional development topics.
  • Client meetings and presentations: Hosting clients at your home for product demonstrations, sales presentations, or relationship-building events.
  • Company holiday or appreciation events: Annual holiday parties, employee appreciation events, or team-building activities that serve a business purpose of improving morale and retention.
  • Investor or partner meetings: Meetings with potential investors, strategic partners, or advisors to discuss business opportunities.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The Augusta Rule is simple in concept, but several common mistakes can undermine the strategy:

  • Exceeding 14 days: If you rent your home for even one day beyond the 14-day limit, all rental income becomes taxable and must be reported on Schedule E. There is no partial exclusion. Staying at or below 14 days is critical.
  • Inflated rental rates: Charging $5,000 per day for a modest three-bedroom home in a market where comparable venues charge $500 per day is not defensible. The rental rate must be supported by market data.
  • No genuine business purpose: Renting your home to your business for a "meeting" at which no actual business is conducted, with no agenda, no minutes, and no attendees other than yourself, will not survive scrutiny.
  • Sole proprietorship use: The Augusta Rule works best when the business entity is separate from the homeowner. A sole proprietor renting to himself raises assignment of income and economic substance concerns. The strategy is most effective with S-Corps, C-Corps, and multi-member LLCs where the entity has its own legal identity.
  • Lack of written agreement: Operating without a formal rental agreement weakens the arrangement. The IRS expects arm's-length transactions to be documented with written contracts, just as they would be between unrelated parties.

Interaction with Other Tax Provisions

The Augusta Rule interacts with several other areas of the tax code that business owners should understand:

Home Office Deduction

You can use both the Augusta Rule and a home office deduction or accountable plan reimbursement for the same home, but they cannot overlap on the same days or the same spaces. The home office deduction or reimbursement applies to the space used regularly and exclusively for business. The Augusta Rule applies to the spaces rented for occasional business events (which may be different rooms than the home office). Careful documentation of which spaces are used for which purpose on which days prevents overlap issues.

S-Corp Considerations

When an S-Corp pays rent to its shareholder, the payment is a deductible expense for the S-Corp, reducing the income that flows through on the K-1. The rental income is excluded from the shareholder's return under Section 280A(g). The net effect is a reduction in the shareholder's taxable pass-through income without a corresponding increase in reportable rental income.

Self-Employment Tax

Rental income that is excluded under Section 280A(g) is not subject to self-employment tax. Since the income is not reported at all, there is no FICA or SE tax implication for the homeowner.
