Why Retirement Plans Are a Business Owner's Best Tax Tool

Retirement plan contributions are among the most powerful tax deductions available to business owners. Unlike many other strategies that defer, shift, or restructure income, qualified retirement plan contributions provide an immediate, dollar-for-dollar deduction that reduces your current taxable income. The contributions then grow tax-deferred (or tax-free in Roth accounts) until retirement.

What makes retirement plans uniquely valuable for business owners is the ability to design a plan that maximizes contributions for the owner while managing costs for any employees. With the right structure, a business owner earning $300,000 or more can potentially shelter over $100,000 per year in retirement plan contributions, reducing their federal and state income tax bill by $40,000 to $50,000 annually.

Solo 401(k): The Foundation for Owner-Only Businesses

The Solo 401(k), also known as an Individual 401(k) or One-Participant 401(k), is the most flexible and powerful retirement plan for business owners with no full-time employees other than themselves and their spouse. It combines the high contribution limits of a traditional 401(k) with the simplicity of a plan that does not require annual compliance testing (as long as plan assets remain under a certain threshold).

Contribution Limits for 2026

The Solo 401(k) allows two types of contributions:

  • Employee elective deferrals: Up to $23,500 in 2026 ($31,000 if you are age 50 or older, with the additional $7,500 catch-up contribution). Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, participants ages 60 to 63 can make an enhanced catch-up contribution of $11,250, bringing the employee deferral to $34,750 for those in that age range.
  • Employer profit-sharing contribution: Up to 25% of W-2 compensation (for S-Corp and C-Corp owners) or approximately 20% of net self-employment income (for sole proprietors and partners), subject to the overall annual addition limit of $70,000 for 2026.

Combined, the maximum total contribution for a Solo 401(k) participant under age 50 is $70,000 in 2026. For those age 50 and older, the maximum is $77,500. For those ages 60 to 63, the maximum reaches $81,250.

Roth Option

Many Solo 401(k) plans now offer a Roth elective deferral option. Roth contributions do not provide a current-year tax deduction, but qualified distributions in retirement are completely tax-free. For business owners who expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement or who want to diversify their tax exposure, the Roth option within a Solo 401(k) is a valuable tool.

A 45-year-old S-Corp owner paying herself $150,000 in W-2 compensation can contribute up to $61,000 per year to a Solo 401(k): $23,500 in employee deferrals plus $37,500 in employer profit-sharing (25% of $150,000).

SEP IRA: Simplicity with High Limits

The Simplified Employee Pension (SEP) IRA is the easiest retirement plan to establish and maintain. There is no annual filing requirement (no Form 5500), no compliance testing, and setup can be completed by the tax filing deadline, including extensions.

How SEP IRA Contributions Work

SEP IRA contributions are made entirely by the employer. There is no employee elective deferral component. The maximum contribution is the lesser of 25% of compensation or $70,000 for 2026. For self-employed individuals, the effective contribution rate is approximately 20% of net self-employment income after the self-employment tax deduction.

The simplicity of the SEP IRA comes with a significant limitation for businesses with employees: if you contribute for yourself, you must contribute the same percentage for all eligible employees. An employee is generally eligible if they are at least 21 years old, have worked for you in at least three of the last five years, and have earned at least $750 in compensation during the year.

SIMPLE IRA: For Small Businesses with Employees

The Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees (SIMPLE) IRA is designed for businesses with 100 or fewer employees. It allows both employer and employee contributions and is less expensive to administer than a traditional 401(k).

SIMPLE IRA Contribution Limits

  • Employee elective deferrals: Up to $16,500 in 2026 ($20,000 for those age 50 and older).
  • Employer contributions: Either a dollar-for-dollar match of up to 3% of compensation, or a 2% non-elective contribution for all eligible employees.

The SIMPLE IRA is best suited for businesses with employees where the owner wants to provide a retirement benefit without the administrative complexity of a 401(k). However, for high-income owners seeking to maximize their own deferral, the SIMPLE IRA's lower contribution limits are a significant drawback compared to the Solo 401(k) or defined benefit plans.

Defined Benefit Plans: Maximum Tax Deferral

For business owners seeking the largest possible tax deduction, defined benefit plans offer contribution levels that far exceed any defined contribution plan. A defined benefit plan promises a specified monthly benefit at retirement, and the annual contribution required to fund that promise is determined by an actuary based on the owner's age, compensation, and the plan's investment returns.

How Much Can You Contribute?

Defined benefit plans can allow annual contributions ranging from $100,000 to over $300,000, depending on the owner's age and compensation. The older you are, the higher the allowable contribution because there are fewer years to fund the retirement benefit. The maximum annual benefit that can be funded is $280,000 per year at retirement age (for 2026), and actuarial calculations determine how much must be contributed each year to reach that target.

Practical Example

A 55-year-old business owner earning $400,000 could potentially contribute $200,000 or more per year to a defined benefit plan. At a combined federal and state marginal tax rate of 45%, this produces an annual tax savings of approximately $90,000. Over ten years, the total tax-deferred savings could exceed $2,000,000.

Defined benefit plans are the most powerful tax deferral tool available to high-income business owners over age 45. Annual contributions of $150,000 to $300,000 are common, producing tax savings of $60,000 to $120,000 per year.

Cash Balance Plans: The Hybrid Approach

A cash balance plan is a type of defined benefit plan that looks and feels more like a defined contribution plan. Each participant has a hypothetical "account balance" that grows by an annual contribution credit and an interest credit. The contribution credit is typically a flat dollar amount or a percentage of compensation, and the interest credit is usually tied to a fixed rate or a market index.

Why Cash Balance Plans Are Popular

Cash balance plans have become the most popular type of defined benefit plan for small business owners because they combine the high contribution limits of traditional defined benefit plans with the portability and transparency of defined contribution plans. The account balance is easy to understand, and the plan can be designed to allow contributions at specific dollar levels that are tailored to the owner's tax planning needs.

Cash balance plans are often "stacked" with a 401(k) profit-sharing plan. This combination allows the business owner to contribute the maximum to the 401(k) ($70,000 in 2026) plus the defined benefit amount (potentially $100,000 to $200,000 or more), creating total annual deferrals that can exceed $250,000.

The Mega Backdoor Roth Strategy

The mega backdoor Roth is a strategy that uses after-tax contributions to a 401(k) plan combined with in-plan Roth conversions to move large amounts into Roth accounts. This strategy can add an additional $23,500 to $46,500 in Roth contributions per year beyond the standard elective deferral limits.

How It Works

The overall annual addition limit for a 401(k) plan is $70,000 in 2026. This limit includes employee deferrals, employer contributions, and after-tax employee contributions. If your total pre-tax and employer contributions are $60,000, you can make an additional $10,000 in after-tax contributions. Those after-tax contributions can then be immediately converted to Roth, either through an in-plan Roth conversion or a rollover to a Roth IRA.

The advantage is significant: the converted amount grows tax-free forever, and qualified distributions in retirement are completely free from income tax. For business owners who have already maximized their pre-tax contributions and want to build additional tax-free wealth, the mega backdoor Roth is an excellent complement to their overall plan.

Plan Design Strategy: Matching the Plan to Your Situation

Choosing the right retirement plan depends on several factors specific to your business and personal financial goals:

  • Owner-only business, under age 50: A Solo 401(k) with profit-sharing typically provides the best combination of high limits and low administrative cost.
  • Owner-only business, over age 50: A Solo 401(k) combined with a cash balance or defined benefit plan can produce total annual deferrals of $150,000 to $300,000.
  • Business with employees, cost-conscious: A SEP IRA provides simplicity, but the equal contribution requirement can be expensive. A 401(k) with a safe harbor match may be more cost-effective.
  • Business with employees, maximum owner deferral: A cross-tested or new comparability profit-sharing plan allows different contribution rates for different employee groups, enabling higher contributions for owners and key employees while meeting nondiscrimination requirements.
  • Late career, catching up: Defined benefit and cash balance plans allow the largest contributions for older owners who need to build retirement savings quickly while generating maximum current-year tax deductions.

Important Deadlines

Establishing a retirement plan requires attention to specific deadlines:

  • Solo 401(k): The plan must be established (document signed) by December 31 of the tax year. Contributions can be made up to the tax filing deadline, including extensions.
  • SEP IRA: Can be established and funded up to the tax filing deadline, including extensions. This is the only plan that can be fully set up retroactively.
  • SIMPLE IRA: Must be established by October 1 of the tax year.
  • Defined benefit and cash balance plans: Must be established by December 31 of the tax year. Contributions can be made up to the tax filing deadline, including extensions (typically September 15 or October 15 with extension).

If you are approaching year-end and have not yet established a retirement plan, the Solo 401(k) (by December 31) and SEP IRA (by filing deadline) are your primary options. For defined benefit plans, planning should begin in Q3 to allow time for actuarial design and document preparation.
