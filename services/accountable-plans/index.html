Accountable Plans: Tax-Free Reimbursements for Business Owners

An accountable plan lets your business reimburse you for legitimate expenses like home office, vehicle use, and travel, completely free of income tax and payroll tax. It is one of the most overlooked strategies in small business tax planning.

Set Up Your Accountable Plan

What Is an Accountable Plan?

An accountable plan is a formal arrangement under which a business reimburses its employees (including owner-employees) for business expenses that the employee incurs on behalf of the company. When the plan meets the IRS requirements under IRC Section 62(c) and Treasury Regulation 1.62-2, the reimbursements are excluded from the employee's taxable income and are not subject to FICA payroll taxes, FUTA taxes, or income tax withholding.

For S-Corp and C-Corp owners who pay personal expenses that benefit the business, an accountable plan converts what would otherwise be non-deductible personal expenditures (since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act eliminated unreimbursed employee business expense deductions through 2025) into legitimate, tax-free reimbursements from the corporation. The corporation gets a deduction, and the shareholder-employee receives the reimbursement without any tax consequences.

The Three IRS Requirements

For a reimbursement plan to qualify as "accountable" under IRS rules, it must satisfy three conditions. Failure to meet any one of these requirements causes the entire reimbursement to be treated as taxable compensation.

1. Business Connection

The expense must have a clear business connection. It must be an expense that would be deductible under IRC Section 162 (ordinary and necessary business expenses) if the employee were paying it directly. The employee must have incurred the expense while performing services as an employee of the company.

2. Adequate Accounting

The employee must provide the employer with adequate documentation of the expense within a reasonable time. Under the IRS safe harbor, expenses must be substantiated within 60 days of when they were paid or incurred. Documentation should include receipts, invoices, mileage logs, and a description of the business purpose for each expense. For meals and travel, additional details including the date, location, business relationship of attendees, and specific business purpose must be recorded.

3. Return of Excess Amounts

If the reimbursement exceeds the actual expense, the employee must return the excess to the employer within a reasonable time. Under the IRS safe harbor, excess amounts must be returned within 120 days of the date the expense was paid or incurred. This requirement prevents the plan from being used to funnel additional compensation disguised as reimbursements.

An accountable plan that meets all three requirements produces a double benefit: the corporation deducts the reimbursement as a business expense, and the employee receives the payment tax-free. There is no payroll tax on either side.

Eligible Expenses Under an Accountable Plan

A properly structured accountable plan can reimburse a wide range of business expenses. The following are among the most common and most valuable for business owners.

Business Use of Home (Home Office)

If you use a dedicated portion of your home regularly and exclusively for business, your S-Corp or C-Corp can reimburse you for a proportionate share of your housing costs. This includes mortgage interest or rent, property taxes, homeowner's insurance, utilities, repairs and maintenance, and depreciation (for homeowners). The reimbursement is based on the percentage of your home used exclusively for business. For example, if your home office occupies 300 square feet of a 2,000-square-foot home, 15% of qualifying housing costs can be reimbursed.

For a business owner with $3,000 per month in qualifying housing costs and a 15% business-use percentage, the annual reimbursement is $5,400. At a combined marginal tax rate of 40% (federal plus state), this produces $2,160 in annual tax savings.

Vehicle Expenses

Business use of a personal vehicle can be reimbursed through an accountable plan using either the actual expense method or the standard mileage rate. For 2026, the IRS standard mileage rate provides a straightforward per-mile reimbursement that covers fuel, insurance, maintenance, depreciation, and other vehicle costs.

Under the actual expense method, the business reimburses the actual costs of operating the vehicle (fuel, insurance, repairs, depreciation, registration, and lease payments) multiplied by the business-use percentage. This method often produces higher reimbursements than the standard mileage rate for vehicles with high operating costs.

Regardless of the method used, a contemporaneous mileage log is essential. The log must record the date of each business trip, the starting and ending odometer readings (or total miles), the destination, and the business purpose. The IRS is particularly strict about vehicle expense substantiation, and a missing or incomplete mileage log is one of the most common reasons for disallowance of vehicle deductions.

Travel Expenses

Business travel expenses are fully reimbursable under an accountable plan. This includes airfare, lodging, rental cars, meals (subject to the applicable percentage limitation), ground transportation, baggage fees, and incidental expenses incurred while traveling away from home overnight for business purposes. The reimbursement must be for expenses with a clear business purpose, and personal days added to a business trip should not be included in the reimbursement.

Cell Phone and Internet

If you use your personal cell phone and home internet for business, the business-use portion of these costs can be reimbursed. The business-use percentage should be documented based on actual usage patterns. For a business owner who uses their cell phone 70% for business, the corporation can reimburse 70% of the monthly phone bill tax-free.

Professional Development

Expenses for continuing education, professional certifications, industry conferences, trade publications, and professional association memberships that maintain or improve skills used in the business are reimbursable. These expenses must relate to the employee's current trade or business, not qualify the employee for a new profession.

Additional Eligible Expenses

  • Business-related books, software, and subscriptions
  • Client entertainment and business meals (subject to percentage limitations)
  • Supplies and equipment used for business at home
  • Business gifts (up to $25 per recipient per year under IRC Section 274(b))
  • Protective clothing and uniforms required for business
  • Tools and equipment not provided by the employer

How to Set Up a Compliant Accountable Plan

Setting up an accountable plan requires a formal written plan document adopted by the corporation's board of directors (or the managing member, for LLCs taxed as corporations). The plan document should include the following elements:

  1. Statement of purpose: A clear declaration that the plan is established to reimburse employees for business expenses incurred in the course of their employment.
  2. Eligible expenses: A description of the categories of expenses eligible for reimbursement.
  3. Substantiation requirements: A statement that employees must submit adequate documentation (receipts, mileage logs, expense reports) within 60 days of incurring the expense.
  4. Return of excess policy: A statement that employees must return any reimbursement amounts exceeding actual documented expenses within 120 days.
  5. Reimbursement process: The procedure for submitting expense reports and receiving reimbursement.
  6. Board resolution: A resolution adopted by the board of directors authorizing the plan and designating the categories and limits of reimbursable expenses.

The written plan document is your audit defense. Without it, the IRS can reclassify every reimbursement as taxable wages, triggering income tax, payroll tax, penalties, and interest.

Accountable Plans vs. Non-Accountable Plans

When a reimbursement arrangement does not meet the three IRS requirements, it is classified as a "non-accountable plan." Non-accountable plan reimbursements are treated as additional taxable compensation. They are included in the employee's W-2, subject to federal and state income tax withholding, and subject to FICA taxes (both employer and employee portions).

The difference in tax treatment is dramatic. Consider a $10,000 annual reimbursement. Under an accountable plan, the full $10,000 reaches the employee tax-free, and the corporation deducts the $10,000 with no payroll tax liability. Under a non-accountable plan, the $10,000 is included in the employee's W-2, and at a 35% combined marginal rate, the employee keeps only $6,500. The corporation also pays its share of FICA (7.65%), adding $765 to the cost.

Special Considerations for S-Corp Shareholders

S-Corp shareholders who own more than 2% of the company face unique rules for certain fringe benefits under IRC Section 1372. However, accountable plan reimbursements are not treated as fringe benefits. They are treated as ordinary business expense reimbursements, and they are available to more-than-2% S-Corp shareholders on the same tax-free basis as they are to any other employee.

This distinction is important. While health insurance premiums paid for a more-than-2% shareholder must be included in W-2 income (with a corresponding above-the-line deduction), accountable plan reimbursements for home office, vehicle, travel, and other business expenses are not subject to the same W-2 inclusion rule. They remain fully tax-free when the plan requirements are met.

Documentation Best Practices

Strong documentation is the key to maintaining accountable plan compliance. We recommend the following practices:

  • Digital receipt capture: Use a mobile app to photograph and categorize receipts immediately. Faded or lost receipts are a leading cause of expense disallowance.
  • Monthly expense reports: Submit formal expense reports monthly rather than waiting until year-end. This ensures the 60-day substantiation window is met consistently.
  • Detailed mileage log: Maintain a daily mileage log with trip date, destination, purpose, and miles driven. Apps like MileIQ or similar tools can automate this process.
  • Separate reimbursement payments: Issue reimbursement checks or transfers separately from payroll. Combining reimbursements with salary payments can create confusion and weaken the plan's documentation trail.
  • Annual plan review: Review and update the accountable plan annually to ensure it reflects current expense categories, reimbursement rates, and IRS guidelines.

Quantifying the Total Savings

For a typical business owner with a home office, a vehicle used 60% for business, regular travel, and cell phone and internet costs, the total annual accountable plan reimbursements can range from $15,000 to $30,000 or more. At a combined tax rate of 40%, this produces $6,000 to $12,000 in annual tax savings for the shareholder-employee, plus additional FICA savings of $1,148 to $2,295 for the corporation (7.65% of the reimbursement amount that would have been taxable as wages without the plan).

These savings recur every year with minimal ongoing costs. The initial plan setup is a one-time effort, and the ongoing compliance requirement is simply maintaining good records, something every business owner should be doing regardless.
