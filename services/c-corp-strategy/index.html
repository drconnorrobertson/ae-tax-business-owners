C-Corp Strategy: The 21% Flat Rate Advantage

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act set the C-Corp tax rate at a flat 21%, creating new planning opportunities for business owners who understand when the C-Corp structure outperforms pass-through entities.

Why C-Corp Strategy Deserves a Fresh Look

For decades, conventional wisdom in tax planning directed nearly all small business owners toward pass-through entities such as S-Corps, partnerships, and LLCs. The reasoning was simple: C-Corps faced double taxation, first at the corporate level and again when profits were distributed as dividends. That calculus changed dramatically in 2018 when the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) reduced the C-Corp tax rate from a graduated scale topping out at 35% down to a permanent flat rate of 21%.

Today, a business owner in the top individual bracket of 37% may find that a C-Corp structure creates a meaningful tax deferral opportunity. When the business does not need to distribute all of its profits to the owner, the spread between the 21% corporate rate and the 37% individual rate represents a 16-percentage-point deferral advantage on every dollar retained inside the corporation.

Understanding the Double Taxation Question

The most common objection to C-Corp status is double taxation: the corporation pays tax on its income, and shareholders pay tax again when profits are distributed as dividends. However, this concern is often overstated for business owners who plan strategically.

Reducing or Eliminating Double Taxation

Several mechanisms can significantly reduce or eliminate the double tax layer:

  • Salary payments: Reasonable compensation paid to shareholder-employees is deductible by the corporation, removing that portion of income from corporate-level taxation entirely.
  • Fringe benefits: C-Corps can deduct the cost of fringe benefits (health insurance, life insurance, disability, and more) that are not available on a pre-tax basis to S-Corp shareholders owning more than 2%.
  • Retirement plan contributions: Employer contributions to qualified retirement plans are deductible at the corporate level.
  • Retained earnings: Profits retained in the business are taxed only at 21%. If the business reinvests in growth, equipment, real estate, or other assets, the second layer of tax is deferred indefinitely.
  • Qualified dividends: When dividends are eventually paid, they are taxed at preferential qualified dividend rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%, not at ordinary income rates.

When salary, fringe benefits, retirement contributions, and retained earnings are factored in, the effective double tax rate for a well-planned C-Corp is often lower than the effective rate on a pass-through entity at high income levels.

Section 1202: The QSBS Exclusion

One of the most powerful benefits available exclusively to C-Corp shareholders is the Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) exclusion under IRC Section 1202. This provision allows shareholders to exclude up to 100% of the gain on the sale of qualified small business stock, up to the greater of $10 million or ten times the adjusted basis of the stock.

Requirements for QSBS Treatment

To qualify for the Section 1202 exclusion, several conditions must be met:

  • The stock must be issued by a domestic C-Corp with gross assets not exceeding $50 million at the time of issuance and immediately after.
  • The stock must be acquired at original issuance in exchange for money, property (other than stock), or services.
  • The shareholder must hold the stock for at least five years.
  • During substantially all of the holding period, at least 80% of the corporation's assets must be used in the active conduct of a qualified trade or business.
  • Certain industries are excluded, including professional services (health, law, engineering, accounting, consulting, financial services), banking, insurance, farming, mining, and hospitality.

QSBS Example

A business owner forms a C-Corp with a $100,000 initial investment. Over seven years, the business grows and is eventually sold for $5,000,000. Under Section 1202, the entire $4,900,000 gain could be excluded from federal income tax. At the 23.8% combined capital gains and net investment income tax rate, this exclusion saves $1,166,200 in federal taxes. This is a benefit that is completely unavailable to S-Corp shareholders.

C-Corp Fringe Benefits

C-Corps offer fringe benefit advantages that are not available to shareholder-employees of S-Corps. Under IRC Section 1372, S-Corp shareholders who own more than 2% of the company are treated as partners, not employees, for fringe benefit purposes. This means they cannot receive certain benefits on a tax-free basis.

Benefits Available Tax-Free to C-Corp Shareholder-Employees

  • Health insurance premiums: The corporation deducts the premiums, and the shareholder-employee receives the coverage tax-free. In an S-Corp, more-than-2% shareholders must include health insurance premiums in W-2 income (though they get an above-the-line deduction on their personal return).
  • Group term life insurance: Up to $50,000 of group term life insurance under IRC Section 79 is excludable from income for C-Corp shareholder-employees.
  • Disability insurance: Employer-paid disability premiums in a C-Corp are excludable from the employee's income.
  • Medical expense reimbursement plans: Under IRC Section 105(b), a C-Corp can establish a medical expense reimbursement plan that covers out-of-pocket medical costs for employees and their families. The corporation deducts the reimbursements, and the employee receives them tax-free. This is particularly valuable for business owners with significant medical expenses.
  • Dependent care assistance: Up to $5,000 per year in employer-provided dependent care assistance under IRC Section 129.

For a business owner with a family health plan costing $30,000 per year plus $10,000 in out-of-pocket medical costs, the C-Corp fringe benefit treatment can save $10,000 to $15,000 annually compared to the S-Corp treatment of the same benefits.

Retained Earnings Strategy

The 21% flat rate makes the C-Corp an excellent vehicle for retaining and reinvesting profits. If your business generates more cash than you need for personal living expenses, retaining those earnings inside a C-Corp means they are taxed at 21% rather than at your personal marginal rate of up to 37% (plus state income taxes and the 3.8% net investment income tax).

Accumulated Earnings Tax Warning

Business owners should be aware of the accumulated earnings tax under IRC Section 531. This is a penalty tax of 20% imposed on corporations that accumulate earnings beyond the reasonable needs of the business for the purpose of avoiding shareholder-level tax. The first $250,000 of accumulated earnings ($150,000 for certain personal service corporations) is generally considered within the reasonable needs of the business. Beyond that threshold, the corporation should document specific, definite, and feasible business plans for the use of the accumulated earnings.

Legitimate business reasons for accumulating earnings include planned expansion, equipment purchases, debt repayment, working capital needs, and self-insurance reserves. We help our clients document these business purposes to protect against accumulated earnings tax challenges.

C-Corp vs. S-Corp: A Side-by-Side Comparison

Choosing between C-Corp and S-Corp status depends on your specific financial situation, business goals, and cash flow needs. Here is how the two structures compare across key dimensions:

  • Tax rate on retained earnings: C-Corp pays 21% flat; S-Corp passes through at individual rates up to 37%.
  • Dividend taxation: C-Corp dividends are taxed at 0% to 20% qualified rates; S-Corp distributions are generally tax-free (to the extent of basis).
  • Self-employment tax: Neither C-Corp nor S-Corp shareholders pay self-employment tax on distributions (both require reasonable salary).
  • Fringe benefits: C-Corp shareholder-employees receive full tax-free treatment; S-Corp more-than-2% shareholders face limitations.
  • Section 199A deduction: S-Corp shareholders may qualify for the 20% qualified business income deduction; C-Corp income does not qualify.
  • Exit planning: C-Corp shareholders may qualify for QSBS exclusion under Section 1202; S-Corp shareholders cannot.
  • Loss utilization: S-Corp losses pass through to shareholders (subject to basis, at-risk, and passive activity rules); C-Corp losses stay at the entity level.

When C-Corp Strategy Makes the Most Sense

The C-Corp structure tends to produce the best results in these scenarios:

  1. High-income owners who reinvest profits: If you do not need to distribute all business profits for personal use, the 21% rate on retained earnings provides a significant deferral advantage.
  2. Businesses planning for sale: If you anticipate selling the business within five to ten years and can meet the QSBS requirements, the Section 1202 exclusion can save millions in capital gains taxes.
  3. Owners with high medical expenses: The ability to establish a medical reimbursement plan under Section 105(b) can generate thousands in additional tax savings annually.
  4. Businesses seeking outside investors: C-Corps offer more flexibility with multiple classes of stock, unlimited shareholders, and structures that are familiar to venture capital and private equity investors.
  5. Companies building enterprise value: When the goal is to build long-term value inside the business rather than to extract current income, the C-Corp's lower rate on retained earnings supports faster compounding.

Converting from S-Corp to C-Corp

If you determine that C-Corp status is advantageous, the conversion process is straightforward. An S-Corp can revoke its S election by filing a statement with the IRS, signed by shareholders holding more than 50% of the outstanding shares. The revocation can be effective as of the beginning of the current tax year (if filed by the 15th day of the third month) or as of the beginning of the next tax year.

However, be aware of the five-year waiting period under IRC Section 1362(g): once an S election is revoked, the corporation generally cannot re-elect S-Corp status for five tax years without IRS consent. This makes the decision an important one that should be modeled carefully before execution.
