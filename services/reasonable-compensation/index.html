Setting Reasonable Compensation for S-Corp Owners

Getting your S-Corp salary right is the foundation of compliant tax planning. Too low and the IRS reclassifies your distributions. Too high and you lose the tax savings. Here is how to find the defensible middle ground.

Why Reasonable Compensation Matters

Every S-Corp shareholder who performs services for the corporation is required to receive reasonable compensation before taking any distributions. This is not a suggestion or a best practice. It is a legal requirement enforced by the IRS under IRC Section 3121(a) and repeatedly upheld in Tax Court and federal district court cases.

The reason is straightforward: FICA taxes fund Social Security and Medicare. When an S-Corp shareholder takes all business profits as distributions rather than salary, the government loses payroll tax revenue. The IRS has made enforcement of reasonable compensation a priority, and audits targeting S-Corp shareholder compensation have increased significantly in recent years.

Setting the right compensation level is a balancing act. You want to pay yourself enough to satisfy the IRS and withstand audit scrutiny, while keeping the salary at a level that preserves the legitimate tax savings of the S-Corp structure. This is where professional analysis becomes essential.

The IRS Standard: What Is "Reasonable"?

The Internal Revenue Code does not define a specific formula or dollar amount for reasonable compensation. Instead, the IRS and the courts have developed a facts-and-circumstances test based on multiple factors. The most commonly cited factors come from a series of court decisions and IRS guidance documents:

Key Factors in Determining Reasonable Compensation

  • Training and experience: The shareholder's education, professional certifications, and years of experience in the field directly influence what comparable employees would earn.
  • Duties and responsibilities: The scope and complexity of the work the shareholder performs for the corporation. An owner who handles sales, operations, and financial management performs multiple roles that would require significant salary if filled by employees.
  • Time and effort devoted: A shareholder working 60 hours per week should receive more compensation than one working 20 hours per week, all else being equal.
  • Comparable compensation: What do similar businesses pay employees for similar work? This is often the most heavily weighted factor. The IRS looks at Bureau of Labor Statistics data, industry salary surveys, and comparable company compensation.
  • Dividend history: If the corporation has never or rarely paid reasonable salaries but regularly distributes large amounts as dividends, the IRS views this as a red flag.
  • Compensation agreements: Written employment agreements that establish salary amounts and the basis for determining them carry weight in an audit.
  • Compensation paid to non-shareholder employees: If your employees earn $80,000 for roles with less responsibility than yours, paying yourself $40,000 is difficult to defend.
  • Economic conditions and the company's financial performance: A highly profitable company is expected to pay higher compensation than a marginally profitable one.

There is no single "safe harbor" percentage or dollar amount. The IRS evaluates each situation individually, which is why a documented compensation study is the most important piece of protection you can have.

Landmark Court Cases Every S-Corp Owner Should Know

Several court cases have shaped the legal landscape around reasonable compensation. Understanding these decisions helps business owners appreciate the risk of getting this wrong.

David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States (2012)

This is the most frequently cited case in reasonable compensation disputes. Watson was an accountant and partner at a CPA firm. His S-Corp paid him a salary of $24,000 per year while distributing approximately $200,000 annually. The court upheld the IRS reclassification and determined that reasonable compensation should have been approximately $91,044 per year based on comparable CPA salaries. Watson was assessed additional employment taxes, penalties, and interest on the difference.

JD and Associates v. United States (2012)

A staffing company's sole shareholder paid himself zero salary while taking $900,000 in distributions. The court ruled that the IRS was justified in reclassifying a significant portion of the distributions as wages. The court noted that the shareholder was the primary revenue generator for the business and that his services had substantial economic value.

Radtke v. United States (1990)

An early and influential case where a shareholder-employee of an S-Corp received zero salary and took all income as dividends. The Seventh Circuit held that amounts paid to the shareholder for services performed were, by definition, wages subject to FICA taxes, regardless of what the payments were labeled.

Sean McAlary Ltd. Inc. v. Commissioner (2013)

A real estate broker's S-Corp paid him $24,000 in salary while distributing over $200,000. The Tax Court increased the reasonable compensation to $83,200 based on comparable broker compensation data. The case reinforced that comparable industry compensation is a primary benchmark.

The Consequences of Getting It Wrong

The penalties for paying unreasonably low compensation can be severe and go well beyond simply paying the unpaid FICA taxes:

  • Reclassification of distributions as wages: The IRS can reclassify all or part of your distributions as compensation, triggering both the employer and employee portions of FICA taxes.
  • Accuracy-related penalty: A 20% accuracy-related penalty under IRC Section 6662 may be assessed on the underpayment of tax resulting from the reclassification.
  • Late deposit penalties: If payroll taxes should have been deposited throughout the year, failure-to-deposit penalties under IRC Section 6656 can apply.
  • Interest: Interest accrues on underpaid taxes from the original due date, compounding the total cost.
  • Trust fund recovery penalty: Under IRC Section 6672, responsible persons (including the shareholder) can be held personally liable for the withheld portion of FICA taxes, even if the corporation cannot pay.

In a typical reclassification scenario, a shareholder who understated compensation by $80,000 could face $12,240 in additional FICA taxes (employer and employee combined), plus a $2,448 accuracy-related penalty, plus late deposit penalties, plus interest. The total cost frequently exceeds the original tax savings by a wide margin.

How to Establish and Document Reasonable Compensation

The best defense against an IRS challenge is a well-documented reasonable compensation analysis conducted before setting the salary amount. Here is what a thorough analysis includes:

Step 1: Job Description and Duties Analysis

Document every role and function you perform for the corporation. Most owner-operators fill multiple roles: CEO, sales director, operations manager, financial controller, and more. Each role carries its own market value, and the combined value of all roles informs the total compensation figure.

Step 2: Comparable Compensation Research

Gather compensation data from multiple sources, including Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, industry-specific salary surveys, professional association compensation reports, and third-party compensation databases such as RCReports or Salary.com. The goal is to establish a defensible range, not a single number.

Step 3: Adjustments for Geographic Location and Company Size

Salaries vary significantly by region. A business owner in New York City performing the same work as one in rural Iowa would command different compensation in the open market. Adjustments for company revenue, number of employees, and industry sector further refine the analysis.

Step 4: Written Compensation Study Report

Compile the analysis into a written report that can be presented to the IRS in the event of an audit. The report should include the methodology, data sources, comparable positions, adjustments applied, and the final recommended compensation range. This document is your primary audit defense.

Step 5: Annual Review

Reasonable compensation is not a one-time determination. As your business grows, your responsibilities change, and market rates shift, the compensation analysis should be updated annually. An amount that was reasonable three years ago may no longer be appropriate if your company's revenue has tripled.

We recommend that every S-Corp owner have a formal reasonable compensation study updated each year. The cost of the study is a fraction of the potential penalties and back taxes from an IRS challenge.

Finding the Optimal Salary Level

The goal is to set compensation at a level that satisfies the IRS while preserving legitimate tax savings. In practice, this means targeting a salary that falls within the defensible range identified by the compensation study, typically between the 25th and 75th percentile of comparable compensation data.

Practical Guidelines

  • Minimum threshold: As a general rule, shareholder salary should be at least 40% to 50% of the corporation's net income before shareholder compensation, though this varies widely by industry and situation.
  • Consider total compensation: Salary is just one component. Health insurance premiums paid by the S-Corp (which must be included in the shareholder's W-2), retirement plan contributions, and other benefits all count as compensation for purposes of the reasonable compensation analysis.
  • Industry norms matter: Service businesses where the owner is the primary revenue generator generally require higher compensation percentages than capital-intensive businesses where equipment and inventory drive revenue.
  • Do not chase a ratio: The IRS has specifically rejected ratio-based approaches. A 60/40 salary-to-distribution split is not inherently reasonable or unreasonable. The determination must be based on the facts and circumstances of each situation.
