How Much Does an S-Corp Save in Taxes?

By AE Tax Advisors Team | September 2026

One of the most common questions business owners ask is whether switching to an S-Corp will actually save them money. The short answer: for most profitable businesses, the savings are significant. The long answer requires understanding how self-employment taxes work and how an S-Corp restructures your income to reduce them.

The Self-Employment Tax Problem

If you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, every dollar of net business income is subject to self-employment tax. That means you pay 15.3% in Social Security (12.4%) and Medicare (2.9%) taxes on top of your regular income tax. For high earners, the Additional Medicare Tax adds another 0.9% on income above $200,000.

For a business owner earning $300,000, that translates to over $30,000 in self-employment tax alone, before you even consider federal and state income taxes.

How an S-Corp Changes the Math

An S-Corp allows you to split your business income into two categories: a reasonable W-2 salary and shareholder distributions. Only your salary is subject to FICA taxes. Distributions are passed through to your personal return and taxed as ordinary income, but they are not subject to the 15.3% self-employment tax.

The key principle: every dollar you can legitimately classify as a distribution instead of salary saves you 15.3 cents in employment taxes.

Real Savings at Different Income Levels

Business Income: $150,000

As a sole proprietor, your self-employment tax would be approximately $21,194. As an S-Corp with a reasonable salary of $70,000, you would pay FICA taxes of $10,710 on the salary portion. The remaining $80,000 taken as distributions avoids self-employment tax entirely. Your estimated annual savings: approximately $10,484.

Business Income: $300,000

As a sole proprietor, self-employment tax hits approximately $33,922 (factoring in the Social Security wage base cap at $168,600 and continuing Medicare tax above that). With an S-Corp and a reasonable salary of $120,000, your FICA taxes drop to $18,360. The remaining $180,000 in distributions is free from employment tax. Estimated annual savings: approximately $15,562.

Business Income: $500,000

At this income level, sole proprietor self-employment tax would be approximately $39,702. With an S-Corp and a reasonable salary of $175,000, your FICA taxes would be approximately $23,378. Taking $325,000 as distributions generates estimated annual savings of approximately $16,324. The savings percentage decreases at higher incomes because Social Security tax caps at the wage base, but the dollar amount remains substantial.

What You Need to Factor In

S-Corp savings are real, but they come with costs and requirements you should consider:

  • Payroll costs: You will need to run payroll, which typically costs $500 to $2,000 per year through a payroll provider.
  • Tax preparation fees: S-Corps require a separate business tax return (Form 1120-S), which adds $1,000 to $3,000 in preparation costs.
  • Reasonable compensation rules: The IRS requires your salary to be reasonable for your role and industry. Setting it too low invites audit risk.
  • State-specific taxes: Some states impose franchise taxes or minimum fees on S-Corps that can reduce your net savings.
  • QBI deduction impact: Your Qualified Business Income deduction under Section 199A is calculated on the distribution portion, and W-2 wages factor into the limitation. Proper planning ensures you maximize both benefits.

When an S-Corp Is Not Worth It

If your net business income is consistently below $40,000 to $50,000, the administrative costs and compliance burden may eat into your savings. Similarly, if you have significant losses or are in the early startup phase, the S-Corp election may not provide immediate benefits. The break-even point depends on your specific situation, including your state tax environment and how much you spend on compliance.

The Bottom Line

For business owners earning $100,000 or more in net business income, an S-Corp election can save anywhere from $8,000 to $20,000 or more per year in self-employment taxes. Over a five to ten year period, those savings compound into six figures. The key is setting a defensible reasonable salary, running clean payroll, and working with a tax advisor who understands the full picture.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can an S-Corp save me in self-employment taxes?

Most S-Corp owners save between $5,000 and $30,000 per year in self-employment taxes depending on their income level. The savings come from splitting your income between a reasonable W-2 salary (subject to FICA taxes) and distributions (which are not subject to self-employment tax).

What income level makes an S-Corp worth it?

Generally, an S-Corp starts making financial sense when your net business income consistently exceeds $40,000 to $50,000 per year. Below that threshold, the additional payroll costs, tax preparation fees, and compliance requirements may outweigh the self-employment tax savings.

Do S-Corp owners still pay Social Security and Medicare taxes?

Yes, but only on the W-2 salary portion of their income. As an S-Corp owner, you pay the 15.3% FICA tax (Social Security and Medicare) on your reasonable salary. However, distributions taken above your salary are not subject to these employment taxes, which is where the savings come from.
